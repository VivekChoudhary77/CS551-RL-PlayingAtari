Deep Reinforcement Learning for Atari Games

Training and comparing five deep RL algorithms on Pong and BeamRider. Project for CS551 Reinforcement Learning, WPI Fall 2025.

What I Did

I reimplemented and extended the classic "Playing Atari with Deep Reinforcement Learning" setup by training and comparing five deep RL algorithms on two Atari games: Pong and BeamRider. The project uses Stable-Baselines3 and Gymnasium, and all training was done on WPI's Turing GPU cluster.

I built an end-to-end pipeline for training, evaluating, and visualizing deep RL agents. The pipeline supports five algorithms: DQN and QRDQN (value-based, off-policy) and A2C, PPO, and RecurrentPPO (policy-based, on-policy). I chose these to compare how value-based methods that learn Q-functions differ from policy-based methods that learn policies directly. Pong and BeamRider were picked because they differ in complexity: Pong is a simple reflex game with a perfect score of 21, while BeamRider is a more complex shooting game where scores can grow much higher.

Each algorithm was trained for 5 million timesteps with hyperparameters tuned from the Stable-Baselines3 RL Zoo. I used SLURM scripts to run jobs on the Turing cluster, then evaluated each trained model over 10 episodes and recorded gameplay videos. A separate script merges per-episode clips into single videos per algorithm and game. I also wrote visualization scripts that read TensorBoard logs and produce learning curves, bar charts, heatmaps, and sample efficiency plots.

What I Achieved

On Pong, all five algorithms reached near-perfect performance. QRDQN hit the maximum score of 21.0 with zero variance. DQN and PPO both averaged 20.8, A2C 20.1, and RecurrentPPO 19.8. This shows that modern RL methods can solve this game reliably.

On BeamRider, value-based methods clearly outperformed policy-based ones. QRDQN reached a mean reward of 7686, followed by DQN at 6122 and A2C at 4158. PPO and RecurrentPPO lagged behind at 2024 and 400 respectively. The main takeaway is that value-based methods with experience replay scale better on complex games, while policy-based methods work well on simpler tasks but need more tuning or data for harder ones. QRDQN's distributional learning helped on BeamRider but came with higher variance. RecurrentPPO's LSTM did not add much over frame stacking, suggesting that four stacked frames already capture enough temporal structure for these games.
